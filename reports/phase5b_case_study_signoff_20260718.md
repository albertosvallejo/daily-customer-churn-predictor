# Validating an A/B Testing Framework Before Trusting It With Real Customers

**A case study from the Daily Customer Churn Predictor project — VivaMarket Brasil (portfolio project)**

---

## The problem with most "A/B testing frameworks"

Most retention teams build an A/B testing setup, run it on real customers, and find out much later — sometimes never — whether it was actually trustworthy. A framework can look statistically correct and still fail in exactly the situations that matter most: when the effect is small, when the sample is thin, or when a safety guardrail sits right at the edge of its threshold.

This project took a different approach before proposing a single real experiment to real customers: **build the framework, then deliberately try to break it, using scenarios where the right answer was already known in advance.**

That work is Phase 5B of the churn intelligence system. This is the closing account of that phase.

## What Phase 5B set out to do

Phase 5B built a proposal-and-validation framework for retention interventions — copy, timing, channel, and incentive changes — with four explicit goals:

1. Generate intervention proposals grounded in real sector literature, not intuition.
2. Run a statistically rigorous A/B test on those proposals (z-test / Fisher exact, Wilson confidence intervals, Holm-Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons, and a three-arm verdict system).
3. **Validate the framework itself** against 16 synthetic scenarios with known, sealed ground truth — generated by a process isolated from the evaluator, under an opaque naming scheme, so the validation couldn't leak the answer.
4. Make the framework honestly report when it doesn't have enough signal, instead of forcing a winner.

The intent was narrow and deliberate: this phase does not claim that any specific piece of copy performs better with real VivaMarket customers. That requires a real pilot, and is explicitly out of scope here. What this phase can claim, and only this, is that the *method* — once real data exists — is calibrated and ready to use.

## The result: 13 out of 16, and where it broke

The framework was run blind against all 16 scenarios — no visibility into the expected answer. It got 13 right, including all 8 scenarios with an obvious effect. The 3 misses were not random noise scattered across the bench; they landed exactly where a rigorous validation is supposed to find weaknesses:

| Scenario | What went wrong |
|:---|:---|
| `07_umbral` | A safety guardrail designed to break marginally — the framework declared a winner anyway (a false negative on safety) |
| `08_obvio` | A deliberately tiny sample (n=25) meant to force "insufficient data" — reported instead as a confirmed finding |
| `08_umbral` | No real guardrail difference (n=150) — declared "significantly broken" anyway (a false positive) |

Every one of the 16 underlying statistical computations was independently re-verified against the raw scenario data and found numerically correct. The failures didn't come from bad math. They came from a layer above the math: how the framework communicates a conclusion when a guardrail sits near its threshold, or when the sample is too small to say anything with confidence. That distinction mattered for how the fix was designed.

## Fixing two of three, and being honest about the third

Two of the three failures were resolved with a redesigned guardrail — the **Power Guardrail** pattern, the same approach used at Airbnb. Before the guardrail's point-estimate trigger fires, a power check now runs first (Cochran's rule: sample size × threshold ≥ 5). If there isn't enough statistical power to detect a real problem, the framework defers to "insufficient sample" instead of firing a verdict it can't back up. This was verified with 53 out of 53 unit tests and against the real scenario data, and it correctly resolves `07_umbral` without reopening it.

The third failure mode — a residual false-positive pattern affecting two scenarios (`01_obvio` and `03_umbral`), where the observed opt-out rate crosses the safety threshold from pure sampling noise even though the true underlying rate is well below it — was not quietly patched over. Seven alternative fixes were tested against the real data, including a fixed minimum-sample rule and a Wilson-CI/non-inferiority test (the pattern used by Spotify and Eppo). Every alternative that removed this residual reopened the original `07_umbral` safety false negative instead. Trading a known, documented, low-stakes false positive for an unresolved safety false negative was judged the wrong trade.

So this residual is documented as a **consciously accepted limitation**, not an open bug: two scenarios will continue to be flagged as "guardrail broken" even though, on the observed data, they're false alarms caused by sampling noise near the threshold. The only structural fix identified — moving to sequential testing (mSPRT) or a Bayesian approach — is logged as future work, not treated as a blocker.

## Three business decisions, made explicitly and dated

Closing this phase required more than code. It required naming three judgment calls, each with its own trade-off, and confirming them as decisions rather than leaving them implicit:

**Decision A — accept the `01_obvio`/`03_umbral` residual.** As above: the Power Guardrail fixes the safety-critical failure; the remaining false-positive pair is accepted because every tested alternative made the safety problem worse, not better.

**Decision B — ground-truth integrity.** The ground-truth file backing the 16 scenarios was not found in its original location and had to be reconstructed from already-documented spec parameters. Under the ALCOA+ data-integrity framework, a reconstruction cannot, by definition, satisfy the "Original" or "Contemporaneous" principles — so it was never sealed as if it were. Instead it went through a standard remediation process: a documented search confirming no original exists, and a reconstruction traceable back to its spec, permanently labeled as an **accepted reconstruction** — never as "sealed" or "original" data.

**Decision C — partial guardrail coverage.** Of the 8 candidate interventions researched, only 3 (`INT-01`, `INT-02`, `INT-04`) have a risk (opt-out rate) that this framework can measure quantitatively today. The other 5 touch risks the current engine can't quantify — perceived manipulation, social-proof credibility, cumulative communication fatigue. Rather than delaying the whole phase to extend the framework's coverage, the decision was to accept the partial coverage now and require **mandatory manual review before any real pilot** of those 5 interventions — the same escalate-to-a-human pattern the Power Guardrail itself uses when it lacks statistical power.

All three decisions were confirmed by the project author on 2026-07-18, dated and attributable, following the same evidentiary standard applied throughout the project.

## From validated method to a ready pilot

With the statistical framework validated and its limits mapped rather than glossed over, the intervention catalog was translated into a prioritized recommendations report (`reports/phase6_intervention_recommendations_20260718.md`): which interventions to test first, the economic framework behind the prioritization, and a ready-to-execute Tier 1 test design — starting with `INT-02` (personalization) — for the first real pilot with actual customers, once authorized.

## What this phase can honestly claim, and what it can't

This is the part that matters most, and it's stated as plainly as everything above:

**Can claim:**
- The framework correctly detects real conversion differences when they exist.
- It does not manufacture false positives when there's no real difference (after the Power Guardrail fix).
- It recognizes when it lacks sufficient signal instead of forcing a conclusion.
- Its intervention proposals are grounded in cited literature, not intuition.

**Cannot claim:**
- That any specific piece of copy "performs better" with real VivaMarket customers — that requires the real pilot, deliberately out of scope here.
- That real churn or customer lifetime value improves in practice — only that the method, once real data exists, is calibrated and ready.

Keeping that boundary explicit — rather than letting a strong validation result quietly imply a stronger real-world claim than it supports — is the same discipline applied throughout the rest of this project's measurement and governance work.
